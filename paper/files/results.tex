\changed{
\paragraph{Comparison with Supervised Probing Methods}
We additionally compare \method~against a supervised probing baseline following the state-of-the-art methodology of \citet{obeso2025realtimedetectionhallucinatedentities}. Specifically, we train a logistic regression classifier on token-level hidden states extracted from TriviaQA, using the same layer selection strategy as the original work, i.e., extracted from the middle layer. During inference, sequence-level hallucination scores are obtained by aggregating token-level predictions via maximum probability, ensuring sensitivity to localized hallucinated spans.
}
% \changed{
% While the trained probe achieves strong performance on its training domain, we observe that its performance degrades on unseen datasets. In contrast, \method~consistently outperforms the probe on RACE, SQuAD, and Natural Questions, highlighting the advantage of a training-free dependence-based criterion for cross-dataset generalization.
% }
\changed{
Table~\ref{tab:probe_comparison} compares \method~against a supervised probing baseline across four datasets and four large-scale LMs. As expected, probing performs strongly on its training distribution. For example, on TriviaQA, probing achieves AUC$_s$ scores of 85.18 and 84.88 on Llama-3-8B and Llama-3-8B-Instruct respectively, with correspondingly high PCC values, reflecting the benefit of supervised alignment.
}

\changed{\revisionr3{
However, this advantage does not consistently transfer across datasets. On SQuAD, \method~outperforms probing across all four models, with gains exceeding 8 AUC points on Llama-3-8B (76.87 vs.\ 68.79) and 17.27 points on Llama-3-8B-Instruct (80.15 vs.\ 62.88). The NQ comparison is mixed: for example, Gemma-2-9B has lower AUC$_s$ with HIDE than with the probe (79.08 versus 85.97), whereas Gemma-2-9B-Instruct has a small HIDE advantage (85.19 versus 84.75).
}}

\changed{\revisionr3{HIDE requires no labeled examples or detector training. It performs better than the probe on SQuAD, while the NQ comparison is mixed. These results show that it can be useful when labeled hallucination data are unavailable; they do not establish that it is uniformly better under changes in domain.}}


\subsection{Faithfulness Hallucination Detection}
\label{sec:faithful_results}

\revisionr3{On SQuAD and RACE, answers must be supported by the supplied context. HIDE has higher AUC$_s$ than both uncertainty baselines, Perplexity and Energy, in $10$ of the $12$ model/dataset settings (Table~\ref{tab:results_exp}). Its mean relative improvement over the better of these two baselines is $47.4\%$ on SQuAD and $20.1\%$ on RACE.}

\revisionr3{\method's advantage persists when compared to the state-of-the-art multi-pass methods. On SQuAD, for example, \method~achieves an $AUC_s$ score of $80.15\%$ with Llama-3-8B-Instruct, which is an absolute improvement of $8$ percentage points over Eigenscore, the state-of-the-art multi-pass baseline. The improvements are also reflected in PCC values, with \method~outperforming single-pass baselines and matching the best multi-pass alternatives in most settings. This trend is also mirrored on the RACE dataset, where \method~provides consistently robust performance across both base and instruction-tuned variants of all models. Overall, for faithfulness hallucination detection, \method~achieves an average relative gain of $33.7\%$ and $4.2\%$ in $AUC_s$ over the  single-pass and multi-pass baselines, respectively. These benchmark relative gains average the per-model/dataset improvement over the best evaluated uncertainty-based or multi-pass baseline in that cell; the corrected attention comparison below is a separate experiment.}

\subsection{Factuality Hallucination Detection}
\label{sec:factual_results}

\revisionr3{We further evaluate \method~on benchmarks designed to test factuality hallucinations, such as Natural Questions (NQ) and TriviaQA. Here, the outputs may be fluent yet factually unsupported. As shown in Tables~\ref{tab:results_exp} and~\ref{tab:exact_match}, even in the case of factuality hallucinations, \method~has higher AUC$_s$ than both uncertainty baselines in $9$ out of $12$ settings, with an average relative gain of $23.3\%$ in $AUC_s$ scores across models for NQ and TriviaQA. For instance, on NQ, \method~achieves $AUC_s$ score of $79.29\%$ with Llama-3-8B-Instruct, compared to $57.47\%$ for the best uncertainty-based single-pass competitor. The PCC comparison varies across models and datasets.}

Compared to multi-pass methods, \method~is generally competitive and, in some settings, outperforms both Eigenscore and Lexical Similarity. \revisionr3{For example, on NQ, \method~has higher AUC$_s$ than the five unsupervised baselines in Appendix B for all six model variants; PCC comparisons are more mixed.} However, in certain configurations, such as TriviaQA with Llama-3-8B, the advantage of \method~is less pronounced, and Eigenscore achieves marginally higher AUC-ROC and PCC values. A closer examination of these cases suggests that datasets with extremely short or repetitive answers can reduce the discriminative power of representation-based dependence measures. We discuss such limitations in detail in \cref{sec:error_analysis}.


\paragraph{\revisionr3{Matched comparison with attention and norm baselines}}
\label{sec:paired_baselines}
\revisionr3{Table~\ref{tab:paired_s} reports the direct comparison. Panel A reproduces the full-benchmark HIDE values from Appendix B for reference; Panel B evaluates all three detectors on the same answers under Section~\ref{sec:paired_protocol}. Only comparisons within Panel B use matched examples and stopping conditions. Attention has higher AUC$_s$ in seven of eight settings. HIDE performs better on Gemma-2-9B/RACE ($57.93$ versus $51.23$), while attention performs better in all four closed-book factuality settings: Llama/NQ ($84.24$ versus $78.47$), Llama/TriviaQA ($73.68$ versus $57.01$), Gemma/NQ ($67.97$ versus $62.74$), and Gemma/TriviaQA ($66.94$ versus $49.83$). The norm also performs better than HIDE on both TriviaQA settings. Attention to the question is therefore a useful detection signal even without a supplied evidence passage. Appendix~\ref{app:paired_diagnostics} reports ROUGE-L results and confidence intervals for the AUC$_s$ differences.}

\revisionr3{We also compare HIDE with a score based only on the actual number $n$ of selected tokens: $c(n)=(n-1)/n^2$ for $n\geq1$, and zero when no output states are available. This is the value of the adapted score for all-ones Gram matrices before their diagonals are removed. The control closely tracks HIDE (PCC at least $0.9987$), with an absolute AUC$_s$ difference below $0.43$ percentage points in every setting. Thus the number of selected tokens largely accounts for the score in these runs. This limits attributing its predictive performance specifically to nonlinear dependence between representations.}

\input{files/tables/paired_s}

\paragraph{\revisionr3{Interpretation of the baseline comparison}}
\revisionr3{The comparison shows that the choice of detector depends on the setting. On Gemma-2-9B/RACE, HIDE's AUC$_s$ exceeds attention by $6.69$ percentage points (95\% confidence interval $[3.70,9.69]$); it also performs better using ROUGE-L. Attention performs better in the other seven settings. The full benchmark and the probe, dialogue, and ablation experiments show HIDE's performance against their respective baselines. Together, these results support HIDE as one detection option, while showing that HSIC is not necessary to obtain a useful signal in every setting.}

\revisionr3{Attention-based detection is also supported by prior work. Lookback Lens trains a classifier on attention ratios and reports strong detection and transfer results \citep{chuang2024lookbackr3}. Both HIDE and the raw attention baseline used here require no detector training. They measure different quantities: attention weights describe how tokens are combined, while the resulting representations also depend on transformed value vectors \citep{kobayashi2020normr3}. This distinction motivates studying hidden states, but the selected-token-count result prevents us from concluding that HIDE's advantage comes from additional semantic information.}

\revisionr3{HIDE can be computed when hidden states are available without extracting attention weights. This distinction can matter in implementations that do not expose those weights. For example, FlashAttention avoids storing the full attention matrix in GPU memory \citep{dao2022flashr3}. Extracting attention mass can require changes to the implementation, although it could be accumulated without storing the full matrix. Hidden-state extraction also has a cost. We therefore identify a difference in required model access, without claiming a measured speed advantage over attention or an advantage in optimized serving.}

\subsection{Generalization to Conversational Hallucinations}
\label{sec:dialogue_results}
\changed{
To evaluate whether the representational decoupling hypothesis underlying HIDE extends beyond question answering, we examine its performance on the dialogue split of the HaluEval benchmark. In this setting, hallucinations typically manifest as inconsistencies with prior conversational context or unsupported factual claims, rather than incorrect answers to well-defined questions. Dialogue therefore provides a challenging testbed for assessing whether input--output representational dependence remains informative in more open-ended generation scenarios.
}

\changed{
Table~\ref{tab:dialogue_results} reports dialogue hallucination detection performance across six language models, including both base and instruction-tuned variants of Llama and Gemma. Overall, HIDE demonstrates competitive and often strong performance across models, particularly on larger architectures. For instance, on Llama-3-8B HIDE achieves the best $AUC_s$ score, substantially outperforming the uncertainty-based single-pass baselines and matching or exceeding multi-pass methods.
}

\changed{
At the same time, performance varies across models and metrics. On some smaller or instruction-tuned models, simpler signals such as lexical similarity or energy-based scores perform competitively, and in a few cases outperform HIDE on individual metrics. This variability highlights the increased difficulty of dialogue hallucination detection, where surface-level similarity or confidence cues may occasionally align with correctness.
}

\changed{\revisionr3{These results extend the evaluation beyond QA: HIDE is competitive on some dialogue settings without additional supervision or sampled generations, but simpler baselines perform better on others. They show useful detection performance in some conversational cases, without establishing a general advantage.}}


\subsection{Computational Efficiency}
\label{sec:compute_time}
\paragraph{\revisionr3{Comparison with multi-generation baselines}}
\revisionr3{Figure~\ref{fig:time_analysis} reports the six-model, four-dataset comparison under standard HuggingFace inference on an A100-SXM4-80GB. For model $m$ and method $b$, we first average the four dataset-specific mean latencies equally, obtaining $\bar T_{m,b}$. We then report the mean relative reduction}
\revisionr3{\[
R_b=\frac{100}{6}\sum_{m=1}^{6}\left(1-\frac{\bar T_{m,\mathrm{HIDE}}}{\bar T_{m,b}}\right).
\]}
\revisionr3{The mean latency reductions are $50.8\%$ relative to EigenScore, $50.8\%$ relative to lexical similarity, and $53.2\%$ relative to LN-Entropy. The average across these three comparators is $51.6\%$. We use the EigenScore comparison when reporting the $50.8\%$ reduction. These measurements compare one-generation and five-generation methods under standard HuggingFace inference.}

\revisionr3{For Llama-3-8B on SQuAD, base generation takes $1.6169$~s and the complete HIDE pipeline takes $1.8445$~s, adding $0.2276$~s ($14.08\%$). On RACE, base generation takes $1.5021$~s and HIDE takes $1.7107$~s. All differences and percentages are computed before rounding.}

\paragraph{\revisionr3{Measuring the overhead added by HIDE}}
\revisionr3{We evaluate Llama-3.2-3B, Llama-3-8B, Gemma-2-9B, and Gemma-2-27B on 200 fixed seed-42 queries from each of SQuAD and NQ. Measurements use standard HuggingFace Transformers/PyTorch, BF16, eager attention, and one A100 80GB PCIe GPU. After ten warm-up base/HIDE pairs, we collect three measured pairs per query, alternate base/HIDE order, synchronize CUDA around measurements, and verify identical generated token IDs within each pair. Model loading and correctness evaluation are outside the measured intervals. Base latency measures generation without hidden-state capture; HIDE total includes generation with hidden-state capture, keyword extraction, score computation, and associated bookkeeping.}

\revisionr3{These measurements use the benchmark stopping conditions, rather than the first-answer-line conditions of the attention/norm comparison. All eight timing jobs use the same GPU. Other workloads shared the host, and we did not record clock rates or power continuously. The confidence intervals describe variation within the runs; they do not account for all possible effects of host sharing or changes in GPU operating conditions.}

\input{files/tables/timing_revision}

\paragraph{\revisionr3{Measured overhead}}
\revisionr3{Table~\ref{tab:timing_revision} and Figure~\ref{fig:scaling_latency} report the paired timing measurements. We average the three repeats within each query, then average over queries. Incremental overhead is $T_{\rm HIDE}-T_{\rm base}$; relative overhead is $100(\overline T_{\rm HIDE}-\overline T_{\rm base})/\overline T_{\rm base}$, not the mean of per-query percentages. We bootstrap queries 2,000 times and retain all measurements, including negative paired differences.}
\revisionr3{For Llama-3-8B on SQuAD, base generation is $0.9277$~s and HIDE total is $1.1587$~s, giving $0.2310$~s overhead ($24.90\%$). On NQ, the corresponding values are $0.7906$~s, $0.8832$~s, and $0.0925$~s ($11.70\%$). Differences are computed from unrounded measurements. These paired measurements quantify generation overhead on A100 PCIe; they are not substituted into the SXM4 baseline comparison or used to calculate its $50.8\%$ reduction.}

\paragraph{\revisionr3{Model scale and complexity}}
\revisionr3{Gemma-2-27B extends the parameter scale substantially: its SQuAD overhead is $0.1969$~s (95\% CI $[0.1644,0.2365]$), and its NQ overhead is $0.0806$~s ($[0.0751,0.0864]$). These observations support feasibility at the tested scales, not constant absolute overhead across models or datasets. The tested hidden widths range from $3,072$ to $4,608$; increasing parameter count does not imply proportionately increasing hidden width. Generated lengths also differ across models (Appendix~\ref{app:paired_diagnostics}), preventing a controlled parameter-only scaling inference.}

\revisionr3{For fixed effective token count $n$, RBF Gram construction costs $\mathcal O(n^2d)$; the implementation's matrix products add $\mathcal O(n^3)$. Thus the score arithmetic is $\mathcal O(d)$ when $n$ is fixed. This statement does not cover autoregressive generation, sequence-dependent keyword extraction, hidden-state collection, or total model parameter count. Fixed-$n$ arithmetic complexity does not imply constant end-to-end overhead.}

\paragraph{\revisionr3{Implications for optimized serving}}
\revisionr3{A single-generation detector avoids requesting additional sampled responses. Its latency in an optimized engine nevertheless depends on batching, access to hidden states or attention weights, and data movement. These factors affect HIDE, the attention/norm baselines, and multi-pass methods that use internal representations. We did not measure vLLM, PagedAttention, or another optimized serving engine. The standard HuggingFace measurements therefore do not establish a speedup, or a lower bound on speedup, in these systems.}

\changed{
\paragraph{Threshold Selection via Balanced Operating Point}
Selecting an appropriate threshold $\tau$ involves balancing two competing objectives: sensitivity (detecting hallucinated generations) and specificity (avoiding false alarms on faithful outputs). Optimizing for accuracy alone can be misleading, particularly under class imbalance, as it may bias the threshold toward the majority class. To avoid this, we select $\tau$ by maximizing the Geometric Mean (G-Mean) of the True Positive Rate (TPR) and the True Negative Rate (TNR):
\[
\text{G-Mean} = \sqrt{\text{TPR} \times \text{TNR}} = \sqrt{\text{TPR} \times (1 - \text{FPR})}.
\]
We sweep $\tau$ over the range of observed \method~scores on a validation set and select the value that maximizes G-Mean. This criterion penalizes extreme failure in either direction and yields a balanced operating point, even when hallucinations constitute a minority of the data.

\changed{\revisionr3{
\paragraph{Interpreting $\tau$ in Deployment}
The threshold $\tau$ selected during validation defines a default operating point for inference: generations with \method~scores below $\tau$ are flagged as hallucinated. In practice, however, $\tau$ can be adjusted to reflect application-specific risk preferences. In safety-critical domains, such as medical or legal assistance, practitioners may raise $\tau$ to prioritize recall, accepting a higher false positive rate in order to minimize undetected hallucinations. Conversely, in user-experience-sensitive applications, such as creative writing or casual dialogue, a lower $\tau$ may be preferred to reduce unnecessary flags on correct outputs. Accordingly, while the G-Mean--optimized threshold provides a principled and balanced baseline, $\tau$ should be viewed as a tunable operating parameter rather than a fixed constant.
}}
