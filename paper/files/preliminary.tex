\section{Theoretical Foundations}
\label{sec:foundations_hsic}
\revisionr3{HIDE is motivated by the hypothesis that hallucinated outputs may be less strongly coupled to the input in the model's hidden representations. We use HSIC to study this possibility. HSIC measures statistical dependence through kernels, which can capture nonlinear relationships that a geometric comparison such as cosine similarity may miss. We distinguish these properties of HSIC from the behavior of the adapted score used in HIDE.}

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}
\subsection{\revisionr3{Measuring Representational Dependence}}
\revisionr3{HSIC measures dependence between two random variables in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space, where a kernel defines similarities between observations. With suitable kernels, population HSIC is zero if and only if the variables are independent. Appendix A.1 gives the formal definition and assumptions. These properties motivate the method; they do not establish that a low HIDE score proves independence or that independence causes hallucination.}

\subsection{Empirical Estimation}
\revisionr3{In practice, HSIC is estimated from a finite sample. The unbiased estimator described in Appendix A.2 requires at least four observations. Because selected input or output token sets can be smaller, HIDE adapts that estimator to handle short sequences (Section~\ref{subsec:irid_algorithm_score}). This adaptation is biased at finite sample sizes. Its detection performance and its relation to simpler scores are evaluated separately in Sections~\ref{sec:results} and~\ref{sec:ablations}.}
