\section*{Responses to Reviewer 1}

\subsection*{Summary and strengths}
Thank you for recognizing the motivation, single-pass design, evaluation, and ablations. We have retained these experiments and revised the interpretation where the additional comparisons call for it.

\reviewcomment{Weakness 1. Value beyond attention mass}{Would a cheaper attention-mass detector match HIDE? Report attention mass and the input-derived norm as standalone baselines.}
\response The direct comparison shows that attention mass is a strong baseline. HIDE performs better on Gemma-2-9B/RACE, with an AUC$_s$ advantage of 6.69 percentage points (95\% confidence interval [3.70, 9.69]); the advantage also holds with ROUGE-L. Attention performs better in the other seven settings. We therefore present HIDE as one useful detection option, without claiming that HSIC is necessary in every setting.

HIDE uses hidden states without requiring attention weights. This can matter when an implementation exposes hidden states but not attention weights. We discuss this distinction using Lookback Lens, the analysis of attention and value-vector norms by Kobayashi et al., and FlashAttention. Both HIDE and our raw attention baseline are training-free. We do not claim a measured speed advantage over attention or an untested benefit from combining the detectors.

We also report a selected-token-count control, which closely tracks HIDE. This limits attributing the score's performance specifically to nonlinear dependence, and is discussed in the paper.
\location{Section 6.2, ``Matched comparison with attention and norm baselines'' and ``Interpretation of the baseline comparison''; Table 4; Tables F.1--F.3; Limitations.}

\reviewcomment{Weakness 2. Model-dependent magnitude versus precision}{The negative HIDE--norm correlation for Llama reverses sign for Gemma, so the general interpretation is not supported.}
\response We agree and have removed the general inverse interpretation. The main text now reports both models explicitly. Gemma's norm correlations are positive, and the earlier negative Llama/SQuAD correlation is not reproduced under the first-answer-line protocol. We state that the relationship depends on the model, dataset, and evaluation conditions. The QCBW comparison remains a way to examine incorrect answers that are close to the question in topic; it is not treated as causal evidence.
\location{Section 4.4 and Figure 2; Section 5 for the stopping conditions.}

\reviewcomment{Weakness 3. Efficiency and limited scaling evidence}{The efficiency numbers are internally inconsistent, and two nearby hidden dimensions provide limited evidence for substantially larger models.}
\response We corrected the dataset mix-up described in AE1 and identified the two timing experiments clearly. Figure 3 reports the baseline comparison; Figure 4 reports paired overhead measurements for four models, including Gemma-2-27B. We removed the constant-overhead claim and distinguish score complexity from total runtime.
\location{Section 6.4; Figures 3 and 4; Tables 6 and F.4.}

\reviewcomment{Substantive encouragement. AUC/PCC, especially for factuality}{Report attention and norm alongside HIDE, especially on NQ and TriviaQA; discuss settings where HIDE does not retain an advantage.}
\response We report AUC$_s$, PCC$_s$, AUC$_r$, and PCC$_r$ for all three detectors on the same answers. Attention performs better on both NQ and TriviaQA for both models. For example, TriviaQA AUC$_s$ is 57.01 for HIDE versus 73.68 for attention on Llama, and 49.83 versus 66.94 on Gemma. The norm also performs better than HIDE on both TriviaQA settings. The discussion explicitly recognizes attention to the question as a useful signal even without a supplied evidence passage.

For this comparison, generation stops after the first nonempty answer line, avoiding continuations into another question. All three detectors were computed on these same generations. We retain all 16,000 selected records, including capped and empty answers and cases without output states. The sample size was chosen for the available compute time after earlier runs had been inspected; examples were not selected by detector performance. The full-benchmark results remain separate because their stopping conditions differ.

The additional control uses $(n-1)/n^2$, where $n$ is the actual selected-token count, and zero when no output states are available. Its PCC with HIDE is at least 0.9987, and its AUC$_s$ differs by less than 0.43 percentage points in every setting. We report this limitation on interpreting the score as evidence of nonlinear dependence. We also clarify the sampling assumptions of the asymptotic result in Appendix A.3 and make Algorithm 1 and Section 4.3.1 explicit about cached states, keyword matching, and the zero-score fallback, in agreement with the implementation.
\location{Algorithm 1 and Section 4.3.1; Section 5; Section 6.2 and Table 4; Appendix A.3; Appendix F, Tables F.1--F.3; Limitations.}

Answer lengths and timing uncertainty are reported so the scope of the comparison is clear.
